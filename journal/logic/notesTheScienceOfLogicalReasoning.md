## Jerome Bruner Theory of Cognitive Development
Bruner's Theory shows how knowledge is built through experiences. Arguing that culture and language drive this growth. Information here is presented through Bruner's theory and arguments. 

### Three modes (stages) to learning

1. Physical Objects (enactive)
2. Diagrams (iconic)
3. Abstract symbols and equations (symbolic)

### Language
Language is crucial for helping us represent ideas that aren't visual; communicate complex, abstract and advanced thoughts; combine symbols to make new meanings;

This ability is what allows humans go beyond information given, and think creatively, reason, and understand deeper patterns. Teaching methods should follow this. (Metaphors)

Example:
* English speakers think of time horizontally (e.g., "looking toward the future"),
* Mandarin speakers think of time vertically (e.g., "the next month down")
### Education
Students should disover the structure of a subject for themselves, finding connections without being told specifically. Therefore, the goal of education is developing autonomous learners who, "learn how to learn".

Bruner believes that education should build the capacity for **symbolic thinking**. Using other forms to represent and manipulate ideas abstractly.

### 1. Readiness
1. Ready mentally to look for connections compared to facts.
2. Feel motivated and focused to take on new challenges.
3. Willing to explore.

### 2. Spiral Curriculum
1. Revisit core ideas throughout learning
2. Increase depth upon recurring topics
3. Build a strong understanding.
#### Teachers Role
* Offer examples
* Break complex ideas into manageable "bits"
* Encourage critical thinking
### 3. Discovery Learning Theory
1. Students are active, not idly listening. They should organize, test, and apply information beyond blindly absorbing it.
2. Search for connections between parts and ideas.
3. Cognitive Effort is applied. Reasoning, problem-solving, and creativity reshapes and strengthens their mental framework.
4. Students should be ready and motivated. There should be sufficient interest and a good level of difficulty.
### Effective Scaffolding
* Design activities that are challenging but achievable.
* Engaging students and keeping them on task.
* Break goals into steps.
* Proper Encouragement.
* Highlight key points.
* Model examples and strategies.
* Encouraging exploration.
#### 6 Key Functions of Scaffolding.
1. Recruitment: Student should be interested in the task.
2. Degrees of Freedom: Remove irrelevant directions.
3. Direction Maintenance: Student should be engaged. Break the task into subsections.
4. Mark critical features: Highlight key points.
5. Frustration: Stop students from giving up.
6. Demonstration: Provide models and imitations for "possible/partial" solutions.
### Limitations & Strengths
* **Strength:** 
    * Backed by meta-analysis: Guided discovery outperforms other instructions, inquiry works when learners receive guidance (Lazonder & Harmsen, 2016), concrete to abstract teaching sequence improves learning and transfer. (Fyfe et al., 2014)
    * Still shapes curricula today from math to medical education.
    * Cultural dimension
* **Limitation:**
  * Minimal guidance can overload working memory and weak basic-skills mastery (Kirschner, Sweller, & Clark, 2006; Mayer, 2004). Novices can struggle with discovery learning.
  * Language claim is overstated.
  * High demand for teachers. Contingent scaffolding requires continuously diagnosing each learner’s progress (Wood et al., 1976; van de Pol et al., 2010) Which is difficult in whole-class teaching and mostly uneven when applied.
